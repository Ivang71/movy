# Therapy-Practice Billing Survives the Cross-Category Test

The earlier single-category pick holds: percentage-of-collections medical billing for insurance-based therapy group practices, launched in behavioral health, is still the service this team should build around once all seven category notes are scored on the same eight criteria, but it wins by one to two points on a 40-point scale, which is inside the noise of a judgment-based score, and the margin that matters is structural rather than numerical. Across roughly 140 recurring services examined, outcome-tied pricing survived only where the fee is a share of money recovered or collected, a licensed signature, or an insured promise; every service priced to a document, transcript, draft, listing, or dataset has already fallen 80 to 99 percent to an AI product. Within the surviving set, behavioral-health group billing and outpatient-rehab billing (33/40 each after normalization) are the only candidates that combine **monthly recurring fees of roughly $40,000 to $150,000 per client**, owner-operator buyers, no professional license, a supplier base of **1,364 small billing firms with none above 5 percent share**, and industry-level evidence. The nearest rivals, Amazon Vendor Central deduction recovery, tenant-side CAM lease audit, chiropractic billing and merchant-processing-fee audit (32 each), have purer contingency pricing and less AI pressure but are episodic, sell to harder-to-reach mid-market finance buyers, or rest on one or two vendor pages. The cross-category evidence sharpens rather than weakens the thesis: **63 percent of billing companies now charge 7.99 percent or less, 59 percent have not adopted AI, 54 percent expect gross margins of 10 percent or below**, and the $500 million-plus of 2026 revenue-cycle venture money is aimed at hospitals and health systems, which leaves a billing firm that prices at 4 to 5 percent of collections with AI-level delivery cost as the open move. The single biggest unverified threat is Candid Health ($120 million Series D), which lists behavioral-health digital providers as customers and is described by one deal tracker as targeting billing companies; confirming its go-to-market is the first thing to check before committing.

## Only recovered-dollar and signed-output fees hold when building is free

The seven notes converge on one mechanism, and it is the mechanism, not any individual category score, that drives the ranking. Where an AI product performs the whole task and no one bears liability for the output, the price has fallen to 1 to 10 percent of the human service; where the customer is buying a transfer of risk, the pricing model has held even as the cost of delivery collapsed. The cleanest within-firm demonstration is Pilot, whose AI-only bookkeeping tier is **$99 per month** while its human-signed tax filing is **$1,000 to $2,450-plus per year** ([Pilot](https://pilot.com/pricing)). Crosby, an AI-native law firm, charges **$250 to $1,000 per contract with malpractice insurance included**, a 50 to 80 percent discount to attorneys rather than the 98 percent discount of a $19.99-per-month unsigned drafting subscription ([TAMradar](https://www.tamradar.com/funding-rounds/crosby-series-b-60m); [Talking Tree](https://talkingtree.app/freelegalguide/ai-vs-lawyer-cost-comparison/)). SOC 2 Type 2 attestations still cost **$15,500 to $50,000 at specialist CPA firms** because the compliance platform "does not issue or sign" the report ([SOC2Auditors.org](https://soc2auditors.org/soc-2-audit-cost/)). Contingency collections at 10 to 35 percent, property-tax appeals at 25 to 50 percent of first-year savings, medical billing at 4 to 10 percent of collections, and Mercor's 30 percent placement fee all retain their model ([Southwest Recovery](https://www.swrecovery.com/resources/blog/average-collection-agency-fees-2026-costs-commission-rates-explained/); [AppealDesk](https://www.appealdesk.com/compare/best-property-tax-appeal-services); [Neolytix](https://neolytix.com/articles/what-is-the-going-rate-for-medical-billing-services/); [Sacra](https://sacra.com/c/mercor/)).

The labor-market data and the investor playbooks agree on why. Stanford's Canaries update finds employment of 22-to-25-year-olds in highly AI-exposed occupations running **about 19 percent below expected**, with declines "concentrated in occupations where AI usage tends to automate human tasks" and flat or rising employment where AI complements workers ([Stanford Digital Economy Lab](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/news/canariesaug26/)). Emergence Capital's AI-native services playbook names outcomes-based pricing as the first of four price supports and lists revenue-cycle management, insurance claims and customs brokerage as good targets ([Emergence Capital](https://www.emcap.com/thoughts/the-ai-native-services-playbook)); Foundation Capital adds the caveat that outcome pricing needs "consistent, measurable results" and fails where inter-customer variance is high ([Foundation Capital](https://foundationcapital.com/ideas/the-4-6t-services-as-software-opportunity-lessons-from-the-first-year)). Margin expectations have reset: Bessemer and ICONIQ put AI product gross margins at **50 to 60 percent**, not the 80 to 90 percent of SaaS, and the best-evidenced AI-native services margin is Crescendo's 60 to 65 percent at an acquired contact-center BPO ([Digital Applied](https://www.digitalapplied.com/blog/ai-unit-economics-pricing-margins-services-2026-framework); [General Catalyst](https://www.generalcatalyst.com/stories/the-future-of-services)). A services firm moving from the incumbents' sub-10 percent gross margin to 50 to 65 percent is the realistic prize.

The practical consequence is that the brief's first criterion, outcome-tied pricing that holds when AI makes building free, acts as a gate rather than a weight. ## Fifteen outcome-tied survivors, and four sit within two points of the top

Each researcher scored their category on the same eight criteria from 1 to 5, but the calibrations drifted. The healthcare note gave behavioral-health billing a 5 on buyer reach while admitting that vendor-switching rates are unquantified; the finance note scored parcel-audit AI compression as a disqualifying 1 while the sales note scored it 3; the sales note gave Vendor Central recovery a 5 on revenue per client on the strength of a UK vendor's marketing. To rank across notes, every candidate that clears the pricing gate (C1 of 4 or 5) was re-scored on one calibration: revenue per client (C2) loses a point when the fee is episodic rather than recurring, because the brief asks for a recurring service; fragmentation (C4) is 3 where a funded entrant or platform bundle sits adjacent to the small-business segment and 2 where one sits squarely inside it; reachability (C6) is 5 for owner-operators who self-identify online, 3 for mid-market CFO or HR buyers, and 2 for carriers, TPAs or enterprise procurement; AI compression (C7) is 2 where a direct AI product undercuts the service and 1 where it does so at roughly a tenth of the price. An evidence grade records how the numbers were sourced: A for independent or industry-level data, B for several agreeing vendor sources, C for one or two vendor pages or search snippets. Three things stand out from the table. First, the top of the ranking is compressed: six services sit within two points of the leader, and a different but equally defensible calibration would reorder them. Second, the therapy-type billing specialties occupy four of the top seven rows, which means the real decision is between a billing firm and a recovery-audit firm, not between forty services. Third, the recovery audits that match the pricing thesis most purely (Vendor Central, CAM, merchant fee, utility, workers' comp) all lose points on the same two criteria, revenue per client and buyer reachability, because their fee scales with a spend pool that only mid-market clients have and their buyer is a controller or real-estate director rather than an owner who feels the pain weekly.

## The behavioral-health pick still wins, by a structural margin rather than a numerical one

The explicit answer to the brief's question is yes: medical billing for small behavioral-health group practices still wins once compared against every other category, and nothing in the other six notes beats it on the combined criteria. Several rivals beat it on individual criteria, and the honest version of the verdict names them. Vendor Central recovery, CAM lease audit and merchant-fee audit each score higher on pricing purity, since they are pure contingency with no fee absent a recovery, and on AI-compression risk (4 against 3), because no AI product has been found selling those outcomes below contingency rates. Utility audit and workers' comp premium recovery beat it on fragmentation (5 against 3). Subrogation beats it on market size, with **$15 to $20 billion in recoverable subrogation going uncollected annually** ([InsuranceIndustry.ai](https://insuranceindustry.ai/billions-left-behind-how-ai-rewrites-the-economics-of-subrogation/)). If the team weighted "no AI compression" and "no funded entrant" above revenue recurrence and buyer reachability, Vendor Central recovery would move to first. On the brief's stated weighting, which treats all eight criteria as co-equal and asks for a recurring service sold to small and mid-sized businesses, it does not.

What separates therapy billing from the recovery audits is three properties that the scores capture only partly. The first is recurrence: a billing fee arrives every month for as long as the practice submits claims, whereas a CAM audit yields a reconciliation adjustment per cycle, a Vendor Central engagement front-loads a five-year backlog, a utility audit pays a one-time refund plus first-year savings, and a workers' comp mod correction pays once and then nothing until the next audit ([National Lease Advisors](https://nationalleaseadvisors.com/lease-audit/); [Wake Commerce](https://www.wakecommerce.co.uk/blog/amazon-vendor-central-shortage-claims); [AAUBA](https://aauba.org/utility-bill-auditing/); [ModAdvisor](https://www.modadvisor.com/audit)). The second is the buyer: a group-practice owner is identifiable from public directories, feels denials and cash delays personally, and switches billers for documented reasons such as non-transparency on denials, repeated errors and slow response ([360Connect](https://www.360connect.com/supplier-blog/top-5-reasons-physicians-switch-medical-billing-providers/)), whereas the recovery audits sell to a controller who has no budget line and must be convinced the money exists. The third is evidence: the billing case rests on IBISWorld's count of **1,364 US billing businesses with no firm above 5 percent share** ([IBISWorld](https://www.ibisworld.com/industry-statistics/number-of-businesses/medical-billing-services-united-states/)), Grand View's **$6.95 billion US outsourcing market growing to $17.69 billion by 2033 with physician offices the fastest segment at 14.4 percent** ([Grand View Research](https://www.grandviewresearch.com/industry-analysis/us-medical-billing-outsourcing-market)), Heard's survey of 1,950 therapists showing **74.6 percent accept insurance** ([Heard](https://www.joinheard.com/resources/the-heard-2026-financial-state-of-private-practice-report)), and the Tebra survey of billing companies, while the Vendor Central and merchant-fee cases rest on two or three vendor pages each.

The per-client economics clear the $20,000 bar comfortably at group scale and fail it for solos, which fixes the target segment. Behavioral-health billers charge **5 to 8 percent of collections** ([MedicalBillersAndCoders](https://www.medicalbillersandcoders.com/blog/how-much-does-medical-billing-cost/); [EliteMed Financials](https://elitemedfinancials.com/mental-health-billing-services-cost/)); insurers pay roughly **$111 per session** against $159 private pay ([Blueprint](https://www.blueprint.ai/blog/private-practice-costs)), and the median therapist sees 18 clients a week ([Heard](https://www.joinheard.com/resources/the-heard-2026-financial-state-of-private-practice-report)), so a 10-clinician insurance-based group collects on the order of $0.9 to $1.0 million and pays $55,000 to $80,000 a year at incumbent rates, or $36,000 to $50,000 at an undercutting 4 to 5 percent. A solo therapist at the $75,000 median yields about $5,000, which is why SimplePractice's bundled billing at roughly 6 percent with a **$750 monthly minimum** effectively charges solos 9 to 12 percent and why 3 to 4 insurance-based clinicians is the floor for a viable account ([Verimedix](https://www.verimedix.com/simplepractice-billing-outsource-therapists/); [Mentalyc](https://www.mentalyc.com/blog/simplepractice-vs-therapynotes)). Outpatient rehab reaches the same account size with fewer clinicians because a physical therapist generates **$280,000 to $380,000 of net revenue per FTE**, so a three-therapist clinic is a $42,000 to $74,000 account at 5 to 6.5 percent ([Proactive Chart](https://www.proactivechart.com/resources/realistic-pt-productivity-benchmarks-for-outpatient-practices-in-2026/)).

The reason to launch in behavioral health rather than rehab, given that the two tie on score, is the shape of the wedge. Behavioral-health denials run **10 to 16 percent** and by some accounts 12 to 20 percent, nearly double medical-surgical rates, and they are driven by eligibility, authorization and session-limit rules rather than clinical coding ([OmniMD](https://omnimd.com/blog/average-claim-denial-rates-by-specialty/); [blueBriX](https://bluebrix.health/blogs/the-top-reasons-behavioral-health-claims-denial)), which is the profile a non-clinical software team can own; 60 to 70 percent of all denials stem from front-end eligibility and data errors and 65 percent are never resubmitted ([OmniMD](https://omnimd.com/blog/average-claim-denial-rates-by-specialty/)). The dominant EHR bundle in the specialty leaves exactly that work undone: SimplePractice's managed billing covers in-network claims only and excludes out-of-network, EAP, aging receivables, appeals and credentialing, while TherapyNotes offers no managed billing at all ([Verimedix](https://www.verimedix.com/simplepractice-billing-outsource-therapists/); [Mentalyc](https://www.mentalyc.com/blog/simplepractice-vs-therapynotes)). In rehab, by contrast, WebPT is "the most widely adopted EMR in outpatient physical therapy," processes 11 million-plus claims and visits a year, and sells a full RCM service at 6.5 percent of collections, so the incumbent platform already owns the whole bundle ([SoftwareFinder](https://softwarefinder.com/emr-software/webpt); [WebPT](https://www.webpt.com/products/rcm-service)). Behavioral health also carries a live switching trigger: Headway cut therapist pay by up to 30 percent on January 1, 2025, and its effective take is reported at 10 to 15 percent of reimbursement, which pushes groups toward direct in-network billing where a third party is needed ([Mental Wealth Solutions](https://mentalwealthsolutions.org/blog/headway-alma-therapist-pay-cuts-marketplace-platforms); [TherapyDial](https://therapydial.com/providers/setup/alma-vs-headway-payout-rates/)). Rehab, ABA and chiropractic share the narrow-CPT, administrative-denial profile and are the natural second, third and fourth specialties.

The cross-category view also changes how the firm should price and position. The incumbents are weak in a specific way: Tebra found that **59 percent of billing companies have not adopted AI**, 67 percent believe payers use AI to increase denials, and 98 percent now sell add-on services to defend revenue ([Tebra](https://www.tebra.com/theintake/practice-operations/billing-companies/medical-billing-industry-outlook)). Meanwhile the funded AI-native revenue-cycle companies, Adonis ($95 million-plus, Mount Sinai), Akasa (Cleveland Clinic), SmarterDx (85-plus health systems), Commure ($7 billion valuation, 130-plus health systems) and Forus ($3 billion), sell to hospitals and large groups ([AlleyWatch](https://alleywatch.com/2026/03/adonis-ai-healthcare-payments-revenue-cycle-orchestration-platform-akash-magoon/); [RevCycleAI](https://revcycleai.com/deals-and-raises/)); the price-collapse note concludes that this leaves "the thousands of sub-$500K billing companies serving small practices as the segment most exposed to a new AI-native entrant pricing at, say, 3–4% of collections." LunaBill, a four-person YC company with $764,000 of contracted ARR, sells automated payer calls to billing companies rather than to practices, which means a small biller can rent AI call capacity rather than build it ([Y Combinator](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/lunabill)). The firm's position is therefore a biller that charges 4 to 5 percent, undercutting the 5 to 8 percent norm, runs denial management and appeals that the EHR bundles exclude, and keeps a compliance program aligned to the OIG's 1998 guidance for third-party billers, which records a "longstanding concern that percentage billing arrangements may increase the risk of upcoding" and warns that combining percentage fees with marketing services may implicate the anti-kickback statute ([OIG](https://oig.hhs.gov/documents/compliance-guidance/805/thirdparty.pdf)). No state license applies except New Jersey's third-party billing service license, and the firm operates as a HIPAA business associate ([MedRevenueHub](https://medrevenuehub.com/how-to-start-a-medical-billing-business-in-the-usa/)).

## The strongest case against each of the top five

Against behavioral-health group billing, the most dangerous single fact is Candid Health. It raised a **$120 million Series D in July 2026** at a valuation that tripled from its February 2025 Series C, reports 190 percent year-over-year contracted run-rate growth and 180 percent net dollar retention, and its public logo wall includes Mood Health, Ophelia, Little Otter and Mantra, all behavioral-health providers ([HLTH](https://hlth.com/insights/news/candid-health-raises-120m-to-expand-ai-revenue-cycle-management-platform); [Candid Health](https://www.joincandidhealth.com/)). The healthcare researcher reads those logos as venture-backed virtual-care companies rather than independent brick-and-mortar groups, but the RevCycleAI deal tracker describes Candid as targeting "physician groups and billing companies," and no pricing was found ([RevCycleAI](https://revcycleai.com/deals-and-raises/)). Beyond Candid, the segment is squeezed from three sides: the EHR bundle (SimplePractice at about 6 percent), the marketplaces (Headway and Alma absorb the whole billing function for solos and small groups), and rate drift at incumbents (63 percent now at or below 7.99 percent). Solo practitioners, 86.5 percent of the market by the healthcare note's reading of the Heard data, sit below the revenue threshold, so the addressable set is groups only. The OIG's stated concern about percentage arrangements and False Claims Act exposure means a compliance mistake is existential, not merely costly ([OIG](https://oig.hhs.gov/documents/compliance-guidance/805/thirdparty.pdf)). And the group-practice collections figure of roughly $90,000 to $100,000 per clinician is the researcher's arithmetic from session counts and reimbursement rates, not a sourced benchmark.

Against outpatient rehab billing, the case is concentration. WebPT's dominance means most PT clinics already have a 6.5 percent RCM offer inside their EMR, and the average clinic takes in only **$871,000 in receipts**, implying two to three therapists and a thin margin for a third party to undercut ([Tupelo SMB](https://www.tupelosmb.com/blog/buying-a-physical-therapy-clinic-industry-overview)). PT-specific denial causes, the 8-minute rule and missing modifiers, are rule-based and therefore the kind of thing the EMR vendor can automate itself ([MedicalBillingOpportunity](https://www.medicalbillingopportunity.com/resources/weekly-news/medical-billing-specialty-demand-2026/)). No fee or revenue data specific to occupational or speech therapy was found, so the "PT/OT/SLP" framing is partly extrapolation.

Against Amazon Vendor Central recovery, the case starts with capital and ends with policy. SPS Commerce paid **$210 million for Carbon6** in a deal closing February 2025, and Carbon6 already owned chargeguard, a 1P vendor-recovery product, so a well-capitalized incumbent with EDI distribution into the same vendor base exists ([Hackceleration](https://hackceleration.com/labs/review/carbon6); [BusinessWire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20230504005455/en/Carbon6-Extends-Profit-Recovery-Solutions-to-1P-Vendors-with-chargeguard-Acquisition)). The recoverable pool, "93 percent of brands are losing 5 percent of their revenue," is a UK vendor's marketing claim, and that vendor markets to brands above £1 million ([Wake Commerce](https://www.wakecommerce.co.uk/profitguard-amazon-chargeback-recovery)). Nobody could say how many US 1P vendors exist under $10 million in revenue, and 1P vendors have no self-serve community equivalent to the FBA seller forums, so reachability is the weakest criterion. The five-year lookback makes year one lumpy and non-recurring. Most importantly, Amazon's conduct in FBA, where it cut the claim window to 60 days in October 2024, began proactive reimbursements in November 2024 and switched the reimbursement basis to manufacturing cost in March 2025, shows it can shrink a recovery pool by policy inside six months ([eComEngine](https://www.ecomengine.com/blog/fba-inventory-reimbursement)).

Against tenant-side CAM lease audit, the case is that it is a project business dressed as a service. The "3 to 5 percent of occupancy costs" recovery figure comes from audit firms' own marketing ([CommercialLeaseCost](https://commercialleasecost.com/cam-reconciliation-commercial-lease/)); the buyer is a mid-market CFO or real-estate director with slow cycles; offshore shops already compete on the abstraction layer at $5 to $25 per lease ([Moraine CRE](https://morainecre.com/resources/comparisons/lease-abstraction-services)); the licensing score of 5 rests on no source mentioning a license rather than a source confirming none is needed; and landlord pushback requires negotiation skill that a no-domain software team does not have. At a $1 million occupancy tenant, a cycle yields roughly $10,000 to $25,000, so the $20,000 bar requires multi-location tenants of the kind RE BackOffice targets with 50 to 1,000 stores ([REBO](https://blog.rebolease.com/how-cam-reconciliation-helps-retailers-identify-and-recover-overcharges-across-store-locations/)).

Against chiropractic billing, the case is that the headline number is inflated. The **$700,000-plus average practice collections** figure comes from a 2025 Chiropractic Economics survey cited by a billing vendor and includes cash-pay revenue, with no source quantifying the insurance-billed share on which a percentage fee would apply ([SpryPT](https://www.sprypt.com/blog/chiropractic-billing-services)). Solo chiropractors are offered flat fees from about $200 a month, a sign of price compression at the bottom ([EMR Guides](https://emrguides.com/chiropractic-medical-billing-service/)), and no chiropractic-specific market size or AI-entrant data was found, so the market score is inferred from general physician-office growth. Merchant-fee audit, tied at 32, carries a similar warning: every savings figure comes from Verisave's marketing, no firm count or market size exists, and the finance note itself infers that the absence of capital in the niche reflects small per-client revenue rather than an overlooked opportunity ([Verisave](https://verisave.com/how-it-works)).

## Conclusion

The cross-category exercise changes the recommendation less than it changes the reasoning behind it. Viewed from inside healthcare, behavioral-health billing looked attractive because therapists have high denial pain and simple codes. Viewed across all seven categories, it is attractive for a different reason: it is the only outcome-tied service where small businesses already pay a monthly percentage fee large enough to matter, the incumbents are demonstrably weak and un-automated, and the venture capital that could crush a new entrant is pointed at hospitals. The recovery audits that beat it on pricing purity share a structural flaw the brief's criteria only partly capture: their fee is a slice of a one-time spend pool, which means the firm must keep refilling its client book, and their buyer is a finance function that must be persuaded the money exists. A billing fee, by contrast, compounds with every clinician the practice adds.

The decision therefore reduces to one empirical question the researchers could not close, which is what Candid Health is actually selling and to whom. If Candid, or any similarly funded company, is already signing independent 5-to-20-clinician behavioral-health groups at a price below 5 percent, the therapy-billing thesis collapses into a price war the team cannot win, and Vendor Central recovery or CAM lease audit becomes the better bet despite its lumpiness. If Candid is, as its logo wall suggests, a vendor to venture-backed virtual-care companies and to billing companies, then it is a supplier rather than a competitor, and the path is to launch in behavioral health at 4 to 5 percent of collections, build the denial-and-appeals layer the EHR bundles exclude, adopt the OIG compliance program from day one, and add outpatient rehab and ABA as the second and third specialties once the first twenty group practices are live.
